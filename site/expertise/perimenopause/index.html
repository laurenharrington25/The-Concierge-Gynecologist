Expertise · Hormone health

Perimenopause

Evidence-based tools to manage the hormonal disruptions, mood changes, sleep alterations, cycle variations and sexual function changes that mark the transition toward menopause.

What perimenopause is

Perimenopause is the transition leading up to menopause, when the ovaries' production of estrogen and progesterone becomes irregular before it eventually stops. It usually begins in the 40s, though it can start earlier, and it can last anywhere from a few years to a decade. Menopause itself is defined in hindsight: twelve months in a row without a period.

Because hormone levels rise and fall unpredictably during this time, symptoms can come and go, change from month to month, and be hard to connect to their cause. Many women are told their symptoms are "just stress" or that they are too young for hormonal changes. Perimenopause is a real, physiologic transition, and it can be treated.

Common symptoms

  • Changes in the menstrual cycle: periods that are closer together or further apart, heavier, lighter or skipped
  • Hot flashes and night sweats
  • Trouble falling or staying asleep
  • New or worsening anxiety, low mood or irritability
  • Brain fog and difficulty concentrating
  • Vaginal dryness, discomfort with sex or changes in desire
  • Joint aches, fatigue and changes in weight distribution

How Dr. Harrington approaches perimenopause

Care begins with time: a thorough conversation about your symptoms, your cycle, your health history and your family history, and what matters most to you. Perimenopause is usually diagnosed from your age and symptoms rather than a single blood test, because hormone levels fluctuate too much for one result to be reliable. Testing is used thoughtfully, for example to rule out other causes such as thyroid disease or anemia, or when bleeding needs evaluation.

From there, Dr. Harrington builds a personalized plan. Depending on your symptoms and health history, it may include:

  • Hormone therapy, including options that also provide contraception when that is still needed
  • Non-hormonal medications for hot flashes, sleep or mood
  • Treatment for heavy or irregular bleeding
  • Vaginal therapies for dryness and discomfort
  • Guidance on sleep, exercise, nutrition and bone and heart health

In keeping with the practice's philosophy, FDA-approved medications are used whenever one exists for your condition.

What to expect

Most treatment plans are adjusted over time as your body continues to change. Follow-up visits are part of the plan, so treatment can be fine-tuned rather than started and left alone. Care is coordinated with your concierge primary care physician.

Common questions

Am I too young to be in perimenopause?

Perimenopause often begins in the early to mid 40s and sometimes in the late 30s. If your cycle, sleep or mood has changed and you are not sure why, it is worth a conversation.

Do I need blood tests to know I'm in perimenopause?

Usually not. Hormone levels swing widely during perimenopause, so a single result can be misleading. The diagnosis is generally based on your age, symptoms and cycle history, with testing used to rule out other causes when appropriate.
