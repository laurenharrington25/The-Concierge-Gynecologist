## Understanding pelvic pain

Pelvic pain is pain in the lower abdomen and pelvis. It may come on suddenly (acute pain) or last for months (chronic pain). It may be constant or come and go, and it may be linked to your cycle, to sex, to urination or bowel movements, or to none of these.

Chronic pelvic pain is common and often under-treated. Many women see several clinicians before anyone takes the time to look at the whole picture. Because pelvic pain often has more than one contributing cause, it benefits from a thorough, patient approach.

## Possible causes

- Painful periods and endometriosis
- Fibroids and ovarian cysts
- Pelvic floor muscle tension or dysfunction
- Bladder conditions, such as painful bladder syndrome
- Bowel conditions, such as irritable bowel syndrome
- Vulvar and vaginal conditions
- Nerve-related pain
- Infections

## How Dr. Harrington evaluates pelvic pain

Care begins with time: a detailed history of your pain, when it started, what makes it better or worse, and how it affects your life. A gentle, careful examination follows, at your pace and only with your consent. Depending on what is found, evaluation may include tests for infection, a pelvic ultrasound or other imaging.

## A multimodal approach

Pelvic pain often improves most when several treatments are used together. Your plan may include:

- Medications, including hormonal treatments that reduce painful periods
- Referral to pelvic floor physical therapy
- Treatment of contributing conditions such as infection or vulvar skin conditions
- Coordination with specialists, such as urology, gastroenterology or pain management, when needed
- Support for sleep, stress and the other ways chronic pain affects daily life

## When to seek urgent care

Sudden, severe pelvic pain, especially with fever, fainting, vomiting or heavy bleeding, or pain during a possible pregnancy, needs urgent medical attention.

## Common questions

### My tests have all been normal. Does that mean nothing is wrong?

No. Many real causes of pelvic pain, such as pelvic floor muscle dysfunction or nerve-related pain, do not show up on imaging or blood tests. A careful history and examination are often what lead to the answer.

### Is painful sex part of pelvic pain care?

Yes. Pain with sex is often connected to other kinds of pelvic pain, and it is addressed as part of the same plan.
